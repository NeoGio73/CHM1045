1Light, sorted by wavelength

Light travels as a wave, and the distance from one crest of the wave to the next is its wavelength. For the light in this lab that distance is tiny, so it is measured in nanometers (nm): one nanometer is a billionth of a meter. A human hair is about 80,000 nm thick.

The eye responds to wavelengths from about 380 nm to about 770 nm, and it reports wavelength as color: violet at the short end, then blue, green, yellow and orange, and red at the long end. Light with a wavelength shorter than violet is ultraviolet. Light with a wavelength longer than red is infrared. Both are every bit as real as the light you see. Your eyes simply have nothing that answers to them. Further out still, in the same family, are X-rays on the short side and microwaves and radio waves on the long side.

Light also comes in packets, called photons, and the energy of one photon is set by its wavelength:

Ephoton = h cλh = 6.626 × 10−34 J s   c = 2.998 × 108 m/s

Here λ (the Greek letter lambda) is the wavelength in meters, h is Planck’s constant and c is the speed of light. Because the wavelength is on the bottom of the fraction, a shorter wavelength means a photon with more energy. That one relation is the key to the whole lab. Slide along the scale below to see it.

2Three kinds of spectrum

Spread light out by wavelength and you have its spectrum. In 1859 Gustav Kirchhoff sorted every spectrum he could make into three kinds, and the sorting still stands.

An unbroken band

Something hot and dense, a solid or a liquid, gives off every wavelength at once. Spread out, its light is one band of color with no gaps.

The glowing element of a toaster. Molten steel. Hot coals.

Bright lines

A thin gas whose atoms have been given energy gives off only certain wavelengths. Spread out, its light is a few sharp lines with darkness between them.

A flame colored by a pinch of salt. The glow of the northern lights.

Dark lines

Shine an unbroken band through a cooler gas and the gas takes out the very wavelengths it would give off if it were glowing. The band comes through with thin dark lines across it.

Sunlight, which has passed through the Sun’s own outer gases.

Look at where the bright lines in the middle strip stand and where the dark lines in the right-hand strip stand. They are in the same places, because the same (imaginary) gas made both. The lines belong to the gas. No two elements give the same set, so a set of lines names an element as surely as a fingerprint names a person. That is the idea behind everything in part 7.

In the lab you will look at two sources of light through a spectroscope and decide for yourself which kind of spectrum each one gives.

3Why an atom gives lines

An electron in an atom cannot have just any energy. It can have only certain values, called energy levels, the way you can stand on the rungs of a ladder but not between them. Each level is numbered with a whole number n, starting from 1 for the lowest. The lowest level is the ground state; any higher one is an excited state.

Give an atom energy, by heating it, by passing electricity through it or by shining light on it, and an electron can be lifted to a higher level. It does not stay there. Within a fraction of a millionth of a second it falls back to a lower level, and the energy it loses leaves the atom as one photon of light. The energy of that photon is exactly the size of the fall:

ΔE = Efinal − Einitialenergy change of the electron

λ = h c|ΔE|wavelength of the light given off

Since only certain levels exist, only certain falls are possible, and so only certain wavelengths can come out. That is why a glowing gas gives lines and not a band. And since every element has its own set of levels, every element has its own set of lines.

Why the energies are negative

By agreement, an electron that has been pulled completely free of its atom, and is standing still, has an energy of zero. An electron held by the nucleus has less energy than that, so its energy is a negative number. The more tightly it is held, the more negative the number. The ground state is the most negative of all.

When an electron falls, it ends with less energy than it started with, so its energy change ΔE is negative too. The photon carries off the size of that change, |ΔE|, which is positive.

The same atom in numbers

Energies this small are awkward to write out, so in this lab every energy is written as a number times 10−18 joule, and you type only the number in front.

Level, nEnergy of the imaginary atom

Worked example: the fall from level 3 to level 2.

  1. Energy change: ΔE = Efinal − Einitial = (−0.43) − (−0.11) = −0.32 × 10−18 J. It is negative: the electron lost energy.
  2. Wavelength in meters, from the size of the change: λ = (6.626 × 10−34 × 2.998 × 108) ÷ (0.32 × 10−18) = 6.21 × 10−7 m.
  3. In nanometers: multiply by 109, which gives 621 nm. That is between 380 and 770 nm, so this line can be seen. It is orange.

Hydrogen

Hydrogen is the simplest atom there is: one proton and one electron. In 1885 a Swiss schoolteacher, Johann Balmer, found a formula that fitted the wavelengths of the hydrogen lines then known, without knowing why it worked. In 1913 Niels Bohr supplied the reason: a rule that gives the energy of every level of the hydrogen atom from its number n alone. In the lab you will use Bohr’s rule to work out hydrogen’s levels and the wavelength of each fall, and then see whether the lines you measured on the bench are where the rule says they should be.

4How a spectroscope sorts light

To see a spectrum, the light first passes through a narrow slit, so that what goes on is a thin, sharp ribbon of light and not a blur. Then something has to send each wavelength in a different direction. There are two ways to do it, and the bench has both.

Reading the scale

Through the spectroscope you see each line standing on a scale of wavelengths. In the closer view the scale has a long mark at every nanometer and a short mark every 0.2 nm. A line seldom stands exactly on a mark, so judge where it is between two of them and give the wavelength to the nearest tenth of a nanometer. Half a division is 0.1 nm.

5Light the eye cannot see

An atom does not care what your eyes can do. Many of its falls give ultraviolet or infrared light, and through a spectroscope those lines are simply not there. A detector finds them. It looks at one narrow slice of wavelengths at a time, measures how much light arrives, moves on to the next slice, and draws the result as a chart: wavelength along the bottom, strength of the light up the side. A line shows up as a peak.

So finding a line with the detector takes two steps: sweep the whole range to see where the peaks are, then zoom in on each peak until you can place the marker on its top. A line in the infrared makes a wider hill than one in the ultraviolet, and the page allows for that when it checks your reading.

One thing about the lab’s detector is too good to be true, and the lab page says so as well. Light of the shortest ultraviolet wavelengths is taken in by glass and even by air, so no detector standing on an open bench could see it. Real measurements there are made inside a vacuum.

6At the bench

  • The tubes. Glass tubes, each holding a little of one gas at low pressure: hydrogen, five other gases, and one marked with a question mark, which is your unknown. They lie in a tray.
  • The power supply. It holds one tube upright and puts about 5000 volts across it. That is what gives the atoms their energy.
  • The white lamp. An ordinary lamp with a hot wire inside, to compare with the tubes.
  • The slit, the prism and the screen. Some of the light passes through them and lands on the screen, spread out by color. This part is for looking, not for measuring.
  • The spectroscope. Click it to look through. It has the scale you read wavelengths from, in a wide view and a closer one.
  • The detector. Its chart is under the 3D view. It reaches from the far ultraviolet to the infrared.
  • The switch for the hood’s light.
  1. Switch the power supply off before you touch a tube. On a real bench 5000 volts is dangerous and a tube that has been lit is hot. The bench will not let you change tubes with the supply on.
  2. Change one thing at a time, and know what is lit before you measure. The detector’s chart says what it is looking at.
  3. Read the lines you can see with the spectroscope. Find the rest with the detector.
  4. Do the bench work first if you like, or the calculations first. The page keeps both, and saves your work in your browser as you go.
  5. For the unknown tube, remember part 2: two tubes of the same gas give the same lines, at the same wavelengths. Color to the eye is a hint. Measured wavelengths are evidence.

Words to know

Wavelength (λ).
The distance from one crest of a light wave to the next.
Nanometer (nm).
A billionth of a meter, 10−9 m.
Photon.
One packet of light. Its energy is h c ÷ λ.
Spectrum.
Light spread out in order of wavelength.
Line.
One wavelength given off (or taken in) by an atom.
Energy level.
One of the energies an electron in an atom is allowed to have.
Principal quantum number (n).
The number of a level: 1 for the lowest, then 2, 3 and so on.
Ground state.
The lowest level, n = 1.
Excited state.
Any level above the ground state.
Ultraviolet, infrared.
Light with a wavelength shorter than violet, or longer than red.
Grating.
A plate of fine, evenly spaced lines that spreads light evenly by wavelength.
Spectroscopy.
Finding out what something is, or what it is doing, from its spectrum.

7Where this is used

What you do in this lab, reading what something is made of from its light, is one of the most widely used ideas in science. Nearly everything known about the universe beyond the solar system was learned this way, and so is a good share of what a hospital laboratory, a steel mill or a police laboratory finds out in a day. Choose a field, or browse them all.

Not every use reads the bright lines of single atoms as you will. Molecules have energy levels too (for the stretching of their bonds, for example), and so do atomic nuclei in a magnet. The rule is the same every time: matter gives off and takes in only light whose energy matches a gap between two of its own levels.
