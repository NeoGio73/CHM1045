1The light of hydrogen

New to this? Read the prelab review first: what wavelength is, why an atom gives off lines, how to read the spectroscope and the detector, and where all of this is used.

A glass tube holds a little hydrogen gas. A high voltage across it gives its atoms energy, and they give that energy back as light. Your job is to find out which wavelengths are in that light: the ones you can see, and the ones you cannot.

The detector looks at whatever is lit and shows how strong the light is at each wavelength, from the far ultraviolet to the infrared. Click the chart to place the marker, or drag across a stretch of it to sweep that stretch more finely. A real detector on an open bench could not see the shortest ultraviolet, which glass and air take in; this one can.

  1. Light the tube. Click the hydrogen tube in the tray (or its name): it goes into the power supply. Switch the supply on. Always switch it off before you change tubes: it puts 5000 volts across the tube.
  2. Prism. Some of the light passes through the slit and the prism and lands on the white screen. Look at the screen with the hood light on, then off.
  3. Spectroscope. Click the spectroscope to look through it. A grating inside spreads the light along a scale of wavelengths in nanometers. Slide along the scale; use the closer view to read where a line stands, to the nearest tenth.
  4. White lamp. Switch the hydrogen off and the white lamp on, and look again through the spectroscope. Then switch the lamp off.
  5. Detector. The eye sees only from about 380 to 770 nm. The detector, under the bench view, reaches far beyond both ends.

    3The energy levels of hydrogen

    In 1913 Niels Bohr showed that the lines of hydrogen follow from one simple rule. The electron in a hydrogen atom can have only certain energies, given by

    En = −2.18 × 10−18 Jn2Eqn 1

    where n, the principal quantum number, is any whole number from 1 up. Work out the energy of the first ten levels. Write every one with the power of ten kept as 10−18, so type only the number in front; one is done for you.

    Table 3. Energy of the first ten levels

    This set of levels belongs to hydrogen alone. The levels of every other element are spaced differently, which is why each gives different lines.

    4Energy changes and wavelengths

    When the electron moves from one level to another, its energy changes by

    ΔE = Efinal − EinitialEqn 2

    ΔE = −2.18 × 10−18 J (1nf2 − 1ni2)Eqn 3

    When the final level is lower than the initial one (nf < ni), the electron loses energy and the atom gives it off as one photon of light. The wavelength of that light follows from the size of the energy change, where h is Planck’s constant (6.626 × 10−34 J s) and c is the speed of light (2.998 × 108 m/s):

    λ (meters) = h c|ΔE|Eqn 4

    λ (nanometers) = λ (meters) × 109Eqn 5
