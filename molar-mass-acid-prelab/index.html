1The plan of the lab

You will be handed a white powder: an acid whose name you are not told. Your job is to find its molar mass, the mass in grams of one mole of it. A balance tells you how many grams you have, but no balance can tell you how many moles. For that you need a reaction you can count with: the acid reacts with sodium hydroxide, NaOH, and the volume of NaOH it takes tells you the moles. Grams divided by moles is the molar mass.

There is a catch. To turn a volume of NaOH into moles, you need to know its molarity very exactly, and NaOH cannot be weighed exactly (part 4 says why). So the lab has three parts:

This page goes through each part: what happens in the flask, how the buret is read, how the color tells you when to stop, and the calculations, each with a worked example and practice.

2Acids, bases and neutralization

An acid is a substance that gives away a hydrogen ion, H⁺ (a proton). A base takes one. Sodium hydroxide is a base: in water it comes apart completely into Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions, and each OH⁻ takes one H⁺ to make a molecule of water.

When an acid and a base react, the products are a salt and water. This is a double replacement reaction of a special kind, called a neutralization. Writing the acid as HA (H is the hydrogen it gives away, A is the rest of the molecule):

HA(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaA(aq) + H₂O(l)the general pattern for an acid with one acidic hydrogen

Two real examples, neither of them used in the lab:

HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)hydrochloric acid: the salt is table salt

HC₂H₃O₂(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaC₂H₃O₂(aq) + H₂O(l)acetic acid, the acid in vinegar: only the H written first is acidic; the three in C₂H₃O₂ stay on the molecule

What really happens, with the ions that just watch (the Na⁺) left out, is that hydroxide pulls the hydrogen off the acid:

HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O

So one OH⁻ uses up one acidic H. That one-to-one counting is the whole basis of the lab: count the OH⁻ you add, and you have counted the acidic hydrogens in the flask.

The point where the moles match

The moment when exactly enough base has been added to react with all of the acid, no more and no less, is called the equivalence point. In the box above it came after the sixth OH⁻. Before it, acid is left over and the solution is acidic. After it, the extra OH⁻ has nothing to react with and the solution turns basic. A titration is a way to find that moment and read off how much base it took.

KHP: an acid with one acidic hydrogen

The acid you will use to standardize your NaOH is potassium hydrogen phthalate, KHC₈H₄O₄, called KHP for short. It has five hydrogens, but only one of them is acidic: the H on the –COOH group. The four on the ring are held tightly and never react with NaOH. So one KHP reacts with one NaOH. (Writing the balanced equation for it is question 2a of your handout’s prelab.)

Why KHP makes a good standard

KHP is a solid that can be bought very pure; it does not pull water from the air, so its mass on a balance is the mass of KHP; it is stable for years; and its molar mass is large, 204.22 g/mol, so a sample big enough to titrate weighs half a gram, which an analytical balance measures to four significant figures or better. A substance like this, weighed to make an exact number of moles, is called a primary standard.

3Molarity and making the NaOH solution

Molarity (M) is the number of moles of solute in each liter of solution:

M = moles of soluteliters of solution so a 0.1 M solution holds 0.1 mol in every liter

Rearranged, it gives the two forms the lab uses most:

moles = M × liters liters = molesM

Liters, not milliliters

Buret readings are in milliliters, and molarity is per liter. Always change mL to L first by dividing by 1000: 25.76 mL = 0.02576 L. Forgetting this is the most common mistake in titration calculations, and it makes the answer 1000 times off.

How much NaOH to weigh

To make a solution of a chosen molarity, work out the moles it must hold, then turn moles into grams with the molar mass. For NaOH the molar mass is 22.99 + 16.00 + 1.008 = 40.00 g/mol.

Worked example: 750 mL of 0.15 M NaOH

Read it left to right: milliliters to liters, liters to moles (the molarity), moles to grams (the molar mass). Every unit struck through appears once on top and once underneath, so it cancels, and only g NaOH is left.

The molarity was only given as “0.15”, two significant figures, so about 4.5 g is the right answer, and a rough balance is good enough to weigh it. Part 4 explains why an exact mass would be wasted effort.

4Why the NaOH must be standardized

If you weighed 4.50 g of NaOH and dissolved it, you might expect to know its molarity. You would not, for two reasons.

  • Solid NaOH is hygroscopic: it pulls water out of the air. Pellets left on a watch glass grow wet and sticky within minutes, so part of what the balance weighs is water, and you cannot tell how much.
  • NaOH solutions take up carbon dioxide from the air, which uses up some of the hydroxide: 2 NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O. This is why the handout says to keep the bottle capped.

So the lab makes the NaOH solution roughly, near 0.1 M, and then finds its exact molarity by titrating it against KHP, whose mass can be trusted. Finding the exact concentration of a solution this way is called standardizing it, and the result is a standardized solution. It is the reason Part II comes before Part III: the molar mass of the unknown can only be as good as the molarity of the NaOH.

5The buret

A buret is a long glass tube with a scale on it and a tap at the bottom, the stopcock. It delivers a liquid a drop at a time and tells you, to a hundredth of a milliliter, how much has come out.

Reading it

  • Zero is at the top. A buret measures how much has come out, so its numbers grow downward: 0.00 near the top, 50.00 near the stopcock. Read from the line above the liquid down.
  • Read the bottom of the meniscus, the curved surface of the liquid, with your eye level with it. Looking from above or below shifts the reading.
  • Read to 0.01 mL. The lines are 0.1 mL apart, so the last digit is your estimate of where the meniscus sits between two lines. 23.40 is a reading; 23.4 is not.
  • The start need not be 0.00. Just record it. The volume used is always found by difference.
volume used = final reading − initial reading

For example, 0.84 mL at the start and 24.31 mL at the end: 24.31 − 0.84 = 23.47 mL of NaOH used.

When a titration needs more than one buret-full

Never let the liquid fall below the 50.00 mL line: below it there is no scale. If a titration still needs more, the handout’s way is to stop above the line, take a reading, refill the buret, take a new initial reading, and carry on. Each filling gives its own volume by difference, and the two are added.

49.62 mL − 0.53 mL = 49.09 mL

Second filling (initial 0.31 mL, final 7.85 mL)

7.85 mL − 0.31 mL = 7.54 mL

Total

6Indicators, end point and equivalence point

An acid and NaOH are both colorless in water, and so is the salt they make. So how do you see the equivalence point? With an indicator: a dye added in a couple of drops, whose color depends on the pH. The pH climbs as NaOH goes in, slowly at first and then, near the equivalence point, very steeply: a single drop can carry it up several units. An indicator that changes color inside that steep climb changes it within a drop of the equivalence point.

The point where you see the color change and stop is called the end point. The equivalence point is a fact about the moles; the end point is what you observe. A good indicator, used with care, puts the two very close together.

The lab uses both. Phenolphthalein (Part II) is colorless in acid and turns pink in base; the end point is the first faint pink that stays for 30 seconds while you swirl. Bromothymol blue (Part III) is yellow in acid and blue in base; the end point is green, the color between. Green lasts only a drop or so, which is why the handout calls the second titration more challenging.

How to approach the end point

  1. Far from the end point, each drop makes a flash of color where it lands that vanishes as soon as you swirl. You can let the NaOH run fast, swirling all the time.
  2. As you get closer, the flashes last longer and spread before they fade. Close the stopcock to a slow drip.
  3. When a flash takes a few seconds to go, add one drop at a time, swirling after each.
  4. At the very end, add half drops: let a drop hang on the tip, touch it to the inside of the flask, and rinse it down with a little distilled water from the wash bottle. Water adds no acid and no base, so it changes nothing you are counting.
  5. Stop when the color stays for 30 seconds of swirling, and read the buret.

7From KHP to the molarity of NaOH

At the end point of Part II, the moles of NaOH that came out of the buret equal the moles of KHP in the flask, because they react one to one. You know the moles of KHP from its mass, and you know the volume of NaOH from the buret. That is all you need:

  1. g KHPfrom the balance
  2. ÷ 204.22 g/mol
  3. mol KHP
  4. × 1 mol NaOH / 1 mol KHP
  5. mol NaOH
  6. ÷ L of NaOH
    from the buret
  7. M of NaOH

Worked example

A student weighs 0.5137 g of KHP, dissolves it, and titrates it to a pale pink. The buret read 0.42 mL at the start and 26.18 mL at the end.
Four significant figures: the mass has four, the volume has four. Keep all the digits in your calculator from step to step and round only the answer.

The amount of water does not matter

The KHP is dissolved in “up to 50 mL” of water, and nobody measures that water exactly. It does not matter: the water changes how dilute the KHP is, not how many moles of it there are, and moles are what the NaOH counts. The same goes for the water you rinse the flask walls with.

9Acids with more than one acidic H

Some acids have two or three acidic hydrogens. Such an acid is diprotic or triprotic, and NaOH takes every one of them. Tartaric acid, the acid of grapes (not used in the lab), is diprotic, written H₂C₄H₄O₆. One molecule uses up two OH⁻:

H₂C₄H₄O₆(aq) + 2 NaOH(aq) → Na₂C₄H₄O₆(aq) + 2 H₂O(l)

If you titrate tartaric acid and calculate as if it reacted one to one, you count every acidic hydrogen as a whole molecule. You find twice as many “moles” as there really are, and so a molar mass half the true one: 75.05 g/mol instead of 150.09 g/mol. Written out, each mole of the acid carries two moles of acidic H:

That one-to-one number has a name: the equivalent mass, the mass of acid that supplies one mole of acidic hydrogen.

equivalent mass = molar massnumber of acidic H for an acid with one acidic H, the two are the same

Because the lab calculates one to one, what you actually measure in Part III is an equivalent mass. Your handout’s table at the end gives both numbers for each possible acid for exactly this reason.

10Several trials and one average

Judging a color by eye is never perfect, so each titration is repeated, and the result is the average of the trials that agree. The handout asks for at least three acceptable trials in each table, and for the ones you used to be marked with an asterisk (*).

A trial that disagrees clearly with the others (most often one where the pink went too dark) is left out of the average. You keep its numbers in the table, so that anyone can see what was measured; you just don’t average it in. Never leave out a trial only because it is a little different: small scatter is normal, and leaving out trials to make the numbers look better is not honest.

Significant figures in this lab

  • Analytical balance masses: every digit the balance shows, four places after the decimal point (0.5137 g).

11At the bench

Safety

Sodium hydroxide, solid or dissolved, is corrosive: it damages skin and, very quickly, eyes. Wear your goggles the whole time. Handle the pellets with a spatula, never your fingers; wipe up any spill and rinse your skin with plenty of water if NaOH touches it. Dissolving NaOH warms the water a little; that is normal. Dispose of waste in the containers your instructor names.

Good habits, in the order you will need them

  1. Part I. Weigh the NaOH quickly on the rough balance, dissolve it completely in the 600 mL beaker, pour it into the plastic bottle and cap it. Every titration in Parts II and III uses this one bottle; a second batch would have a different molarity.
  2. Filling the buret. Close the stopcock, fill with NaOH (a funnel helps; take it out afterwards), then run some liquid through to fill the tip, so that no air is left below the stopcock.
  3. Weighing the acid. On the analytical balance, tare the weigh paper first, then add the solid. Record every digit. Tip all of the solid into the flask.
  4. Before each titration, read the buret and write down the initial reading. Then titrate as in part 6: fast, then drops, then half drops, swirling the whole time, on white paper.
  5. Overshot? If the pink is too dark, still record the final reading. That trial may be left out of the average later, but the record shows you did it.
  6. Part III uses 2 to 3 drops of bromothymol blue and only about 0.25 g of acid. Go slower near the end: yellow, then yellow-green, then green. Blue means you have gone past.
  7. Write everything in your notebook as you go: the unknown’s number, each mass, each pair of buret readings.
