Quick reference: choose the naming system first

Ionic

Name the positive ion first, then the negative ion. Use charge balance to write the formula. Keep a polyatomic ion together.

Binary molecular

Prefixes specify atom counts. The second element ends in -ide. Do not reduce a molecular formula.

Acid or hydrate

Use the acid naming pattern when the substance is identified as an acid. For a hydrate, name the salt, then specify its waters of hydration.

In this lab, use the familiar spellings monoxide, tetroxide, and pentoxide. The uncontracted forms tetraoxide and pentaoxide are also accepted. Omit mono- on the first element.

What the numbers mean

Subscript: counts the preceding atom or parenthesized group. In Mg(OH)2, both O and H are doubled.

Roman numeral: gives the metal ion’s charge. Iron(III) means Fe3+, not three iron atoms.

Hydrate coefficient: counts whole H2O units after the dot. The 5 in CuSO4·5H2O contributes 10 H and 5 O.

Acid patterns

Binary acid in water: hydro- + root + -ic acid. HBr(aq) is hydrobromic acid; HBr(g) is hydrogen bromide.

Oxyacid: anion -ate → acid -ic; anion -ite → acid -ous. No hydro- prefix. Sulfate gives sulfuric acid, and phosphate gives phosphoric acid.

Changing a subscript changes the composition. To balance an ionic formula, change the number of whole ions—not the internal subscripts of a polyatomic ion.

Learning goals and instructor notes

Use common introductory naming conventions to move between names and formulas; infer variable metal charges; preserve polyatomic ions; count atoms through parentheses and hydration; distinguish ionic ratios from molecular atom counts.

Suggested sequence: explore one example from each family, balance three ion pairs, then complete a selection of challenges in both directions. This is formative practice with a built-in answer checker, not a secure assessment.

The examples cover introductory inorganic nomenclature. Organic names, coordination compounds, and exceptions to simple charge rules are outside this activity. Acid exercises explicitly request aqueous formulas. Atom counts describe the written acid formula, not the actual distribution of particles after ionization in water.
