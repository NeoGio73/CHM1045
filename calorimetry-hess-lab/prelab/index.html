1The plan of the lab

The goal of the lab is one number: the enthalpy change, ΔH, for burning magnesium metal in oxygen to make magnesium oxide.

Mg(s) + ½ O₂(g) → MgO(s)the target reaction (Rxn 2 in your handout): ΔH = ?

ΔH tells you how much heat a reaction gives off or takes in, per mole. You might think the easy way to find it is to burn some magnesium and measure the heat. It is not.

Why not just burn the magnesium?

  • Too violent. Burning magnesium gives a blinding white flame (never look straight at it) and reaches thousands of degrees.
  • Too fast. It is over in seconds, before heat has spread evenly into anything you could measure.
  • Too hard to catch. Some of the heat leaves as light, and white MgO smoke drifts away. A foam coffee cup would melt or burn.

So the lab goes around the problem. You measure the heat of two gentle reactions that happen in a cup of acid near room temperature. Then you combine them with one reaction whose ΔH is given, using Hess’s law (part 7), to get the ΔH you could not measure directly. This is called finding ΔH indirectly.

The three reactions, as your handout lists them:

Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)Rxn 3: you measure its ΔH in Part II

MgO(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)Rxn 4: you measure its ΔH in Part III

H₂(g) + ½ O₂(g) → H₂O(l)   ΔHf° = −285.84 kJ/molRxn 5: given in the handout

How to arrange these three to get the target is post-lab question 1. That is your work: part 7 teaches the rules on a different example, so you will know how to do it.

In short

  • The lab finds ΔH for Mg + ½ O₂ → MgO, a reaction too violent to measure in a cup.
  • Part I measures how much heat the cup itself takes up (Ccal).
  • Parts II and III measure ΔH for two gentle reactions in acid.
  • Hess’s law combines them with a given reaction to get the target.

2Energy, heat and temperature

These three words are used loosely in everyday life. In chemistry they mean different things.

Energy
The ability to do work or to warm something. It is measured in joules (J); 1000 J = 1 kJ. (One food Calorie is about 4184 J.)
Temperature
How hot or cold something is. It measures how fast its particles move on average. Measured in °C here. A change of 1 °C is the same size as a change of 1 K, so J/°C and J/K are the same unit.
Heat (q)
Energy that moves from one thing to another because their temperatures differ. Heat always flows on its own from the hotter thing to the colder one, never the other way, until both reach the same temperature.

Temperature and heat are not the same. A bathtub of warm water and a cup of boiling water: the cup has the higher temperature, but the bathtub holds far more heat energy, because it has far more water.

System and surroundings

To keep track of energy, split the world in two. The system is the part you are studying. The surroundings are everything else. In a coffee-cup calorimeter:

  • The system is the reaction: the atoms that are rearranging (for example, the solid and the acid reacting).
  • The surroundings are the water the reaction happens in, the cup, the lid, the probe, and (we hope, only a little) the air in the room.

The first law: energy is never made or lost

The first law of thermodynamics says the total energy of the universe stays the same. Energy can move from the system to the surroundings, or back, but whatever one side loses, the other side gains.

ΔEuniverse = ΔEsystem + ΔEsurroundings = 0 Eqn 1

This is the idea behind every calculation in the lab: if you add up every heat, the sum is zero.

The sign of q

q is always written from the point of view of the thing it belongs to:

  • q is positive (+) when that thing gains heat. Its temperature goes up (unless it is melting or boiling).
  • q is negative (−) when that thing loses heat. Its temperature goes down.

Two names for the two directions:

  • Exothermic (“heat goes out”): the system gives heat to the surroundings. qsystem is negative, ΔH is negative, and the water gets warmer. A hand warmer is exothermic.
  • Endothermic (“heat goes in”): the system takes heat from the surroundings. qsystem is positive, ΔH is positive, and the water gets colder. An instant cold pack is endothermic.

Notice the trap: the thermometer is in the surroundings. When the thermometer goes up, the reaction (the system) has given heat away, so its q is negative.

Try it: which way does the heat go?

Thermometer in the water
ΔH of the reaction

In short

  • Heat is energy that flows from hot to cold. Temperature is how hot something is.
  • The system is the reaction; the surroundings are the water, the cup and the rest.
  • All the heats add up to zero (the first law).
  • q > 0 means gains heat; q < 0 means loses heat. Exothermic: water warms, ΔH < 0. Endothermic: water cools, ΔH > 0.

3Specific heat and q = m s ΔT

Put the same pan of water and a same-sized iron pan on a stove: the iron gets hot much faster. Different materials need different amounts of heat to warm up. The specific heat capacity, s, says how much: it is the heat needed to warm 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C.

Water’s specific heat is large: s = 4.184 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ (also written J/(g·°C)). It takes 4.184 J to warm 1 g of water by 1 °C. Most metals and rocks have a specific heat below 1 J/(g·°C), so they warm up far faster than water given the same heat.

The heat a substance gains or loses is:

q = m · s · ΔT Eqn 2 q in J, m in g, s in J/(g·°C), ΔT in °C
ΔT = Tf − Ti always final minus initial, so ΔT has a sign

Because ΔT is final minus initial, q comes out with the right sign by itself: something that cools has Tf < Ti, a negative ΔT, and a negative q. Do not drop the minus sign.

In the lab, the volume of water or of 1.0 M HCl is measured, not its mass. The handout says to take the density as 1.00 g/mL, so 95.0 mL is 95.0 g. It also says to use water’s specific heat for the HCl solution.

Try it: same heat, different materials

Three made-up blocks of the same mass start at 20.0 °C. Each one is given exactly the same amount of heat. Move the sliders and see how far each one warms. ΔT = q ÷ (m · s).

Blocks, J/(g·°C)ΔT, °CTf, °C

The block with the largest s warms the least. Water’s large specific heat is why a cup of water is a good place to catch heat: a lot of heat gives a temperature change that is easy to measure but not wild.

Worked example

75.0 g of water warms from 21.40 °C to 28.65 °C. How much heat did it gain?
  1. ΔT = Tf − Ti = 28.65 − 21.40 = +7.25 °C
  2. q = m s ΔT = (75.0 g)(4.184 J/(g·°C))(7.25 °C) = +2275 J, or +2.28 kJ
The sign is positive: the water gained heat. If it had cooled by 7.25 °C, q would be −2275 J.

Practice: heat gained or lost by water

In short

  • Specific heat s is the heat to warm 1 g by 1 °C. Water: 4.184 J/(g·°C).
  • q = m s ΔT, with ΔT = final − initial, so q carries its own sign.
  • The same heat warms a high-s material less than a low-s one.
  • In the lab, 1 mL of solution counts as 1.00 g.

4The calorimeter and its constant

A calorimeter is a container that catches the heat of a process so it can be measured. In general chemistry it is a coffee-cup calorimeter: foam cups with a lid. Foam is a poor conductor of heat, so most of the heat stays in the liquid and shows up as a temperature change. The cup is open to the air (through the hole in the lid), so the pressure stays constant; that is why the heat measured is an enthalpy change.

The cup is not a perfect insulator

An ideal calorimeter would keep all of the heat in the liquid. A real one does not:

  • The cup takes some heat itself. The inner wall, the lid and the metal probe start at the liquid’s temperature and must warm up with it.
  • Some heat leaks out through the walls and the hole in the lid, slowly, the whole time. That is why the temperature drifts down after the peak (part 5).

The heat the cup takes is handled with one number, the calorimeter constant, Ccal (also called the heat capacity of the calorimeter). It is the heat needed to warm the whole calorimeter by 1 °C, in J/°C. It is like m · s for the cup, rolled into one number because nobody weighs the cup’s parts separately.

qcal = Ccal · ΔT Eqn 3 Ccal in J/°C

Each calorimeter has its own Ccal. Once you have measured yours (Part I), you use it for Parts II and III, in the same cup.

How Part I finds Ccal

Cold water goes into the cup first and sits until the cup and the water are at the same temperature, Tcold. Then hot water (temperature Thot, taken just before pouring) goes in. Heat flows from the hot water into the cold water and into the cup, until all three end at one final temperature, Tf. No reaction happens, so the first law says:

qhw + qcw + qcal = 0 Eqn 4
mhw s ΔThw + mcw s ΔTcw + Ccal ΔTcal = 0 Eqn 5
  • ΔThw = Tf − Thot: negative, so qhw is negative (the hot water lost heat).
  • ΔTcw = Tf − Tcold: positive.
  • ΔTcal = Tf − Tcold too: the cup started at the cold water’s temperature. So ΔTcal = ΔTcw.

Solve in three steps: work out qhw and qcw with Eqn 2; then qcal = −(qhw + qcw) from Eqn 4; then Ccal = qcal ÷ ΔTcal from Eqn 3.

Try it: mix hot and cold water

Choose the waters and a made-up calorimeter constant, then press Mix. The bars show where the heat goes. The hot water’s bar (lost, to the left) is always exactly as long as the other two together (gained, to the right): the sum is zero.

Final temperature Tf
ΔT of the hot water
ΔT of the cold water and cup

Set Ccal to 0 (a perfect cup): Tf lands exactly halfway between the two temperatures when the masses are equal. With a real cup, part of the heat warms the cup, so Tf lands lower.

Worked example (made-up numbers)

50.2 mL of cold water at 21.40 °C is in the calorimeter. 49.8 mL of hot water at 66.20 °C is poured in. The extrapolated final temperature is 42.31 °C. Take 1.00 g/mL and 4.184 J/(g·°C).
  1. Masses: mcw = 50.2 g, mhw = 49.8 g.
  2. qhw = (49.8 g)(4.184)(42.31 − 66.20) = (49.8)(4.184)(−23.89) = −4978 J
  3. qcw = (50.2 g)(4.184)(42.31 − 21.40) = (50.2)(4.184)(20.91) = +4392 J
  4. qcal = −(qhw + qcw) = −(−4978 + 4392) = +586 J
  5. Ccal = qcal ÷ ΔTcal = 586 J ÷ 20.91 °C = 28.0 J/°C
Check the sign: Ccal must come out positive. A negative Ccal means a sign was dropped, or Tf or a starting temperature was misread.

Keep the digits

qcal is a small difference between two big numbers (here 4978 and 4392). Round too early and it changes a lot. Keep every digit in your calculator until Ccal, then round.

Practice: find a calorimeter constant

In short

  • A coffee-cup calorimeter catches heat in a liquid, but the cup takes some heat too.
  • Ccal is the heat to warm the whole calorimeter by 1 °C; qcal = Ccal ΔT.
  • Part I: qhw + qcw + qcal = 0. Find qhw (negative) and qcw, then qcal, then Ccal.
  • The cup’s ΔT is the cold water’s ΔT.

5Reading a temperature–time curve

The probe records the temperature twice a second for 10 minutes. The graph of temperature against time has three parts:

  1. A flat start. About 10 s of readings before anything is added: the starting temperature, Ti.
  2. A quick rise after the second liquid or solid goes in, bending over into a rounded top.
  3. A slow, nearly straight fall, as heat leaks out of the cup into the room.

Why the highest reading is not Tf

The calculations assume all the heat moved instantly, at the moment of mixing, with no heat lost. In a real cup, mixing and reacting take time, the cup’s walls warm up slowly, and heat starts leaking out while the temperature is still rising. So the top of the curve is lower than the temperature an ideal, instant mix would have reached.

The fix: the slow fall after the peak is almost a straight line. Fit a straight line to that cooling region, and extend it back to the time of mixing. The temperature where the line crosses the mixing time is Tf: the final temperature you would have had if everything had happened at once. This is called extrapolating.

Try it: extrapolate the cooling line

A made-up run. Drag across the graph to choose the cooling region (or use the two sliders below it). A least-squares line is fitted to the readings you chose and drawn back to the time of mixing. Point at the graph, or tab to it and use the arrow keys, to examine single readings.

Choosing a good cooling region

  • Start after the curve has become straight, well past the rounded top. A region that includes the top bends the line and gives a wrong Tf.
  • Take a long stretch, a few minutes. A short region has few readings, and the noise tips the line.
  • Extrapolate to the moment of mixing, the time the second reagent went in, not to time zero and not to the top.

Practice: read Tf from a run

°C

In short

  • The curve: flat start (Ti), quick rise, rounded top, slow straight fall.
  • The top is too low, because mixing takes time and heat leaks out meanwhile.
  • Fit a line to the straight cooling part and extend it back to the moment of mixing: that is Tf.
  • The lab accepts a Tf within 0.3 °C of a fit to your own cooling region.

6From heat to enthalpy

In Parts II and III, a reaction happens in the cup. Now there are three heats: the reaction’s (the system), and the solution’s and the cup’s (the surroundings). By the first law, they add up to zero:

qrxn + qsoln + qcal = 0 Eqn 6
so qrxn = −(qsoln + qcal)
  • qsoln = msoln · s · ΔT, where msoln comes from the volume of solution at 1.00 g/mL, and s = 4.184 J/(g·°C).
  • qcal = Ccal · ΔT, with your Ccal from Part I and the same ΔT (the cup starts at the solution’s temperature).
  • ΔT = Tf (extrapolated) − Ti (the flat start).

If the solution warms up, qsoln and qcal are positive, so qrxn is negative: the reaction is exothermic.

Per mole: the enthalpy of reaction

Twice as much solid gives twice as much heat. To get a number that belongs to the reaction itself, divide by the moles that reacted. That is the enthalpy of reaction:

ΔHrxn = qrxnn Eqn 7 change J to kJ (÷ 1000) and report kJ/mol

ΔH has the same sign as qrxn: negative for exothermic, positive for endothermic.

Moles, and which reactant counts

  • From a mass: n = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol).
  • From a solution: n = molarity (mol/L) × volume (L). Change mL to L first: 75.0 mL = 0.0750 L.
  • The limiting reactant is the one that runs out first. Only as much reaction happens as it allows, so the heat comes from its moles. Use the mole ratio of the balanced equation to see which one runs out.

Example: which runs out? (made up)

A made-up solid Y reacts with a made-up acid HA in a 1 : 2 ratio (1 Y uses 2 HA). You have 0.0150 mol of Y and 60.0 mL of 0.80 M HA.
  1. Moles of HA = 0.80 mol/L × 0.0600 L = 0.048 mol.
  2. Y would need 2 × 0.0150 = 0.0300 mol HA. There is more than that (0.048 mol), so the acid is in excess.
  3. Y is limiting. All 0.0150 mol of Y react; ΔH is reported per mole of Y.

In the lab the acid is in excess and the solid is limiting: the handout asks for ΔH per mole of the solid. Check this with your own masses and the moles of HCl in the cup.

Worked example (made-up solid Q)

2.40 g of a made-up solid Q (molar mass 96.0 g/mol) dissolves in 85.0 mL of water in a calorimeter with Ccal = 30.0 J/°C. The temperature goes from 22.10 °C to an extrapolated 26.12 °C. Find ΔH per mole of Q.
  1. ΔT = 26.12 − 22.10 = +4.02 °C
  2. qsoln = (85.0 g)(4.184 J/(g·°C))(4.02 °C) = +1430 J
  3. qcal = (30.0 J/°C)(4.02 °C) = +121 J
  4. qrxn = −(1430 + 121) = −1550 J = −1.550 kJ (exothermic: the water warmed)
  5. n = 2.40 g ÷ 96.0 g/mol = 0.0250 mol Q
  6. ΔH = −1.550 kJ ÷ 0.0250 mol = −62.0 kJ/mol

Practice: ΔH of a made-up solid dissolving

In short

  • qrxn + qsoln + qcal = 0, so qrxn = −(qsoln + qcal).
  • ΔH = qrxn ÷ n, in kJ/mol, per mole of the limiting reactant.
  • Solution warms → qrxn < 0 → ΔH < 0 (exothermic).
  • Moles: grams ÷ molar mass, or molarity × liters.

7Hess’s law

State functions: only the start and the end matter

Two hikers leave the same camp for the same summit. One climbs a steep, direct trail; the other winds over a pass and down a little before the last climb. They walk different distances and get differently tired, but the change in altitude is the same for both: summit minus camp.

A quantity like that is called a state function. Enthalpy is a state function. So ΔH for going from some reactants to some products is the same whether the change happens in one step or in several. That is Hess’s law: if a reaction can be written as the sum of other reactions, its ΔH is the sum of their ΔH values.

Three rules for handling equations

  1. Reverse a reaction → change the sign of ΔH. If A → B gives off 50 kJ (ΔH = −50 kJ), then B → A takes in 50 kJ (ΔH = +50 kJ).
  2. Multiply a reaction by a number → multiply ΔH by the same number. Twice the moles, twice the heat. Halving works too.
  3. Add reactions → add their ΔH values. Anything that appears on both sides of the sum (the same substance, in the same state) cancels, like in algebra.

The aim is to flip and scale the given reactions so that, when they are added, everything you do not want cancels and exactly the target reaction is left.

In short

  • Enthalpy is a state function: ΔH depends only on the start and the end.
  • Hess’s law: if reactions add up to the target, their ΔH values add up to the target’s ΔH.
  • Reverse → flip the sign. Multiply → multiply ΔH. Add → add ΔH; cancel what appears on both sides.

8Systematic and random error

Every measurement differs a little from the true value. Scientists sort the causes into two kinds, because they behave differently and are fixed differently. This part teaches them on other experiments: naming the sources of error in this lab is post-lab question 3, and it is yours.

Systematic error

Pushes every result the same way (always too high, or always too low), usually by about the same amount. It comes from the method or an instrument.

Repeating the measurement does not help: the average is off too. It is found by checking against a known standard and fixed by changing the method or calibrating the instrument.

Example: a balance that reads 0.30 g high on everything.

Random error

Scatters results both ways, a bit high one time and a bit low the next, unpredictably. It comes from limits of reading, timing and small changes you cannot control.

It cannot be removed, but averaging several trials shrinks its effect, because highs and lows partly cancel.

Example: starting a stopwatch a little early or late each time.

Mistakes are not “error” in this sense. Spilling a sample, misreading a scale, writing down the wrong number, or using the wrong formula are gross errors (blunders). The cure is to redo the step, not to name it as a source of error. Your handout says gross errors do not count for points.

Two words go with these: results are accurate when their average is close to the true value (little systematic error), and precise when they agree closely with each other (little random error). Results can be precise but not accurate: tightly grouped, in the wrong place.

    In short

    • Systematic: same direction every time; averaging does not fix it; calibrate or change the method.
    • Random: scatter both ways; averaging more trials reduces it.
    • Mistakes (gross errors) are not sources of error: redo the step.
